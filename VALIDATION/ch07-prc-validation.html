PRC taxi-out — the validation plan

This is the operational plan: the split you rehearse with must look like the test you will be judged on — and here the test is two islands in the future.

The task: predict the taxi-out time (seconds between off-block and takeoff) for departures at 11 big European airports. Training data: every movement of 2025, about 4.17 million rows. Score: one RMSE over departures in January 2026 and July 2026, both months pooled into a single number. The five months between them are not given. Three rules of the game shape everything below:

  • Teams are ranked by their best RMSE across all submissions — no picking two final entries as on Kaggle; every submission stands.
  • There is no hidden final subset (confirmed): the score computed on the ranking file is the final metric. No curtain, no shake-up from a private split — the number you see is the number that decides the prizes.
  • No hard submission limit, but a soft one that bites: organizers watch for leaderboard mining, and teams that queried aggressively in the first days had their best submissions deleted. Submitting at a working rhythm is fine; a 500-probe information campaign is how you lose your best score.
  • External data is allowed if it is openly available and documented (weather is the obvious one for taxi-out).
  • Winners must publish reproducible source code (GPLv3), so the pipeline must be clean enough to show — one more reason to keep validation systematic.
training months (labels given) test months (departure taxi-outs blanked) months that do not exist for you

So validation must answer one question, taken literally: “how well will a model trained on 2025 do on one month right after training, and on one month seven months later?” The book’s golden rule for tuning a validation system (p. 218) says the same thing from the organizers’ side: replicate the way they split the data. They split by time, twice.

Phase 0 · before modeling

Audit the data, write the availability contract

Chapter 7’s leakage section defines the enemy: information in training that won’t be available at prediction time — “posterior” features. The defense is a written contract, produced on day zero and obeyed by every feature afterward. For each column and each derived feature, answer: is this knowable at the moment the aircraft goes off-block?

  1. Map the blanks empirically. Load ranking.parquet and list, for DEP rows, which columns are actually populated. The docs say BLOCK_TIME_UTC_mvt and TAXITIME_SEC_mvt are blanked — check whether MVT_TIME_UTC_mvt (takeoff time!), runway, and stand are present or not. Whatever is populated for test departures is fair game as a feature; whatever is not must never enter the feature set, in training either. Do the same for arrivals — if their times are present (docs imply yes), arrival-based congestion features stay live in the test months.
  2. Classify every _flt column as before-or-after off-block. Flight-plan fields filed pre-departure (EOBT, requested route/level, aircraft type, operator) are safe. Anything the Network Manager updates during or after the flight (actual takeoff time, updated trajectory estimates) is posterior — the book’s credit-score example in aviation clothes. When unsure for a column, compare its value distribution between 2025 DEP rows and ranking DEP rows: a field that is filled in training but empty or frozen in ranking is telling you it is posterior.
  3. Hunt organizer-processing leaks — the book’s historical list applied here: is row order informative (sorted by a hidden time key)? Do IDs increase with time (MVT_ID vs date — if monotone, an ID-derived feature would fake-win in validation and die in a relaunch or embarrass you in the open-code review)? Any duplicated movements from the movement–flight join, and do duplicates straddle months?
  4. Rule for the pipeline, not just the features: the book’s “leaky validation strategy” warning — every fitted transformation (imputation, target encoding, scaling, clustering) fits inside a fold’s training part only. In practice: one sklearn-style pipeline object per fold, nothing fit on the full year.
  5. External data joins get the same contract. Weather (METAR/TAF), NOTAMs, holiday calendars are open and allowed. Join them as-of the off-block moment (observed METAR, not the day’s later observations). Bonus: like arrival features, weather stays perfectly fresh in January and July 2026 — it does not suffer the staleness trap below.
Phase 1 · first week

Measure the gap with adversarial validation

Don’t assume the 2026 months resemble 2025 — measure it. Adversarial validation (pp. 221–226): drop the target, stack training rows with ranking rows, label them 0/1 by origin, and ask a classifier to tell them apart, scoring with out-of-fold ROC-AUC (cross_val_predict + a random forest or GBM is the book’s recipe). AUC ≈ 0.5 means indistinguishable; approaching 1.0 means the test lives elsewhere.

  • Run it three ways: 2025 vs January 2026; 2025 vs July 2026; and January 2026 vs July 2026. The third one tells you whether the two halves of your score are really two different problems.
  • Strip trivial separators first — dates, IDs, and anything monotone with time (the book warns progressive identifiers alone will push AUC to 1.0 and teach you nothing). What you care about is separation through legitimate features.
  • Read the feature importances of the adversarial model: they name which features drift. Expect the stale rolling aggregates and anything traffic-volume-related to rank high.
  • Act with the book’s three levers, in this order: (a) suppression — drop the worst drifters if they matter little to the main model (but the book warns suppression can gut your signal if overdone); (b) validate by mimicking the test set — weight or select validation rows that the adversarial model scores as test-like, a refinement layered on top of the folds below; (c) train on test-like examples — last resort here, since it shrinks a year of data.
  • Re-run the adversarial check whenever the feature set changes shape — it is a monitoring instrument, not a one-off.

Why not just k-fold — the instinct that lies

The book is explicit about when random folds break: “your cases are non-i.i.d., particularly when dealing with time series.” Taxi-out data is non-i.i.d. twice over. Flights from the same morning share one cause (a snowstorm, a closed runway), so random folds let the model “predict” a queue it was shown. And rolling features tie neighbors in time together, so a validation row’s features are partly built from trained-on rows. To size the lie, I built a seeded world shaped like this competition — 6 airports, 30 operators with slowly drifting behavior, shared per-airport-day congestion shocks, seasonality, rush-hour peaks, 18 months long so 2026 has ground truth. The promise misses by 40% for January and almost 2× for July. A validation score that measures memorization, not prediction — the shake-up machine from the chapter’s opening, built at home. And July is the worse month for a structural reason:

Why July is its own trap

Think about what “operator’s mean taxi-out over the last 30 days” can see at prediction time. In training, the window sits right behind each flight. For January 2026, labels end at December 31 — the window is days stale, fine. For July 2026, the freshest departure taxi-out you own is still December 31: February–June were never given, and January’s taxi-outs are blanked because they are the other half of the test. The “last 30 days” feature silently becomes a “seven months ago” feature:

the 30-day window the feature is computed from the flight being predicted taxi-out labels unavailable (not given, or blanked test rows)

Only features built from past departure taxi-outs go stale. Arrival movements keep their times in the ranking months (verify in Phase 0), flight-plan columns are always there, and external weather is always current — those stay fresh. The staleness cost, measured in the seeded world (identical model, only the operator-window freeze date moves):

The rule that follows, and the single most important sentence in this plan: in validation, compute every feature exactly as it will be computable at prediction time. For any fold, features for validation rows are frozen at the training cutoff — never at the row’s own date.

Phase 2 · the standing machinery

The harness

Walk-forward folds (the book’s Figures 7.3–7.5, plus the gap idea behind TimeSeriesSplit(gap=...)): three monthly folds as the fast loop for everyday experiments, and one dress rehearsal per test island:

training months (features per-row, strictly past) validation month (features frozen at training cutoff) feature freeze for the July rehearsal

Layered on top once Phase 1 has run: if adversarial AUC comes back high, re-weight the rehearsal folds toward test-like rows (“validating by mimicking the test set”). No purging or embargo beyond the freeze rule — congestion doesn’t span month boundaries the way overlapping financial returns do; the freeze already removes the only cross-boundary channel (the rolling windows).

The book’s two consistency checks (p. 218) then tune the validation system itself:

  • Internal: are the three monthly folds telling one story? If Sep/Oct/Nov RMSEs scatter wildly, the harness is too noisy to rank ideas — stabilize before trusting it (more folds, repeated runs with different seeds; the bootstrap is the chapter’s tool for a heavy-tailed metric’s standard error, and taxi-out is heavy-tailed).
  • External: does local improvement move the official score the same direction? Every submission gets a log row — local pooled RMSE next to official RMSE — and a running correlation. A systematic offset is fine (it carries information: the 2026 months are harder/easier than the rehearsals); a direction disagreement is a red alert that the harness is missing something the real test contains — stop feature work and investigate.

Did the rehearsals predict the show in the seeded world? Trained once more on all of 2025 and walked into the true 2026 months:

Within about 10 seconds either way, against the naive factor-of-two miss. Honest limitation: the July rehearsal ran a shade optimistic (159 vs 172). It copies the feature staleness but cannot copy everything — the real July model is 7 months old at prediction time, and drift in between is invisible from one year of data. Read the July fold as a slightly rosy floor; expect July to be the harder half of the score.

Phase 3 · every day

Operating protocol

  1. One harness, every idea. Every feature, model, and parameter change is judged on the same fixed folds — one log line per experiment: idea, fold RMSEs, pooled score, seed. The book’s note-everything discipline (p. 219) exists precisely so you can later correlate local scores with official ones and diagnose mismatches.
  2. Pool, don’t average, the headline estimate. The organizers compute one RMSE over January and July together; mirror it by pooling squared errors from the two rehearsal folds weighted by row count (the Chapter 6 fold-averaging trap, avoided by construction).
  3. Slice every score: per airport, per fold, and worst-days. RMSE is dominated by big misses, and taxi-out has heavy right tails (de-icing mornings, single-runway ops). A model that wins the average by failing one airport is a fragile win.
  4. Tune on the monthly folds, decide on the rehearsals. Hyperparameter search never touches Dec or Jul-2025 folds; they stay clean for comparing finished candidates — nested CV’s honesty at a fraction of its cost, exactly the trade the book endorses.
  5. Keep out-of-fold predictions from every run: free error analysis (which hours, airports, weather days hurt) and the raw material for blending/stacking later (Chapter 10).
  6. Re-run adversarial validation after any big feature-set change; watch whether the drifting-feature list grows.
Phase 4 · submissions and endgame

Playing a best-of-N scoreboard with no curtain

The rules are now settled, and they change one thing the book takes for granted. Chapter 7’s “believe your validation score, not the leaderboard” assumes a private test set behind the public one. Here there is none: the visible score is the final metric. So the roles split cleanly. The official score is ground truth — when it disagrees with your folds, it wins, full stop. The harness is your experiment engine — it exists because the oracle is rate-limited by scrutiny, not because it is untrustworthy. You can afford hundreds of local experiments per week and only a handful of official answers; the harness decides what is worth asking.

  • Spend a small, one-time probe budget first. The book’s probing section (pp. 220–221) gives the exact trick for RMSE: submitting a constant c returns RMSE² = variance + (mean − c)², so two constant submissions solve for the test target’s mean and variance, and a third — changing the constant only on January rows, identified via the template IDs — separates the two months’ means. That calibrates the July-drift question with real 2026 numbers before you have any model. Three probes, done once, early, is a working rhythm; what got teams’ scores deleted was sustained mining. Do not run iterative probe campaigns (binary-searching subsets, per-airport constants, and so on) — the marginal information is small and the enforcement risk lands on your best submission, which is your entire ranking.
  • Know the score’s resolution before comparing submissions. Count the rows in submitting.parquet and estimate the RMSE standard error by bootstrapping your rehearsal-fold squared errors at that n (heavy-tailed errors make it wider than the normal approximation suggests — the chapter’s own use case for the bootstrap). Visible differences smaller than that are ties: don’t celebrate them, don’t chase them, and don’t let them overrule the harness when choosing the next experiment.
  • Then one submission per genuine milestone, each logged next to its local pooled score — the external consistency check from Phase 2. A stable offset between the columns is information (it measures how much harder the 2026 months are than the rehearsals — fold it into your expectations). A direction flip means the harness stopped predicting the thing that pays; investigate before the next feature sprint.
  • Endgame: retrain the winning configuration on all of 2025 (settings chosen by the harness; averaged fold models are the fallback if the full retrain can’t be validated), and in the final week submit your top three-to-five candidates as ranked by the rehearsal folds — under best-of scoring, a small diverse portfolio is free insurance against your own local ranking being slightly off. That is the chapter’s closing advice (“use your validation scores when deciding final submissions”) adapted to a scoreboard where nothing is hidden: local scores pick the candidates, the official score crowns one, and there is no private set left to fear.

The harness earning its keep — an idea it killed. A plausible refinement: since July’s features are 6 months stale, train a separate July model whose training features are also lagged 6 months, so it never learns to lean on freshness it won’t have. Sounds right. In the seeded world it scored 182 s vs 172 s for the ordinary model — worse, because lagging threw away more signal than the shift cost. Maybe real data disagrees; the point is the harness answers in one cheap, trustworthy experiment. That is what validation is for.
