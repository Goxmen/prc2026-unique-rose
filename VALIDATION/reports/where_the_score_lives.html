Where the score lives

Validation says 236.32 s. The leaderboard says 279.38 s. The 43.07 s between them has been treated as a flaw in how we validate. Two diagnostics — one over saved out-of-fold predictions, one over 2026 labels we already hold and had never used — say otherwise: the error validation measures and the error the leaderboard charges are two different populations.

Diagnostic 0 · saved out-of-fold predictions, no refit

Validation's error is broad, not concentrated

The expectation going in was that RMSE would be dominated by a handful of catastrophic rows, making any average-over-a-million-rows diagnostic useless. Measured on the v38 recipe's saved predictions, that is wrong.

R_A pools 1,079,528 flights at RMSE 249.9460. Its worst single flight carries 1.05% of the squared error; its worst hundred carry 13.69%; it takes 16,630 flights to reach half. Predict every artifact row exactly — every Rome rule row, every missing flight ID, every label over two hours — and the combined score falls only from 236.32 to 213.78. 81.8% of the squared error survives.

Inside validation, taxi-out prediction is an ordinary regression problem with a modest tail.

The contrast

The leaderboard is not shaped like that

Submission v33 changed nothing but 58 predictions, clamping them to the window the flight plan allows. The official score fell from 309.9872 to 282.4525. Those 58 rows were 16.98% of v30's entire squared error over 344,841 flights, and the run report attributes almost all of it to a single flight — ITY340, moved from 12,930 to 83,160 s.

Validation's worst 58 flights come to roughly 11.7%, and its worst single flight to 1.05%. One ranking row was worth about sixteen of validation's worst. That is not a difference in how well the model fits; it is a difference in what the two populations contain.

Diagnostic 1 · 2026 ground truth we already hold

January 2026 changed its recording regime

ranking.parquet blanks the target only on departure rows. Every one of its 344,693 arrival rows still carries its real in-block time and taxi-in label. That is direct, measured 2026 ground truth about how these airports record movements — no model, no inference from departures — and it had not been used.

Start with departures that carry no flight ID: 1.26% of validation rows, 21.66% of its squared error, RMSE 1,036 s against ordinary flights' 222 s.

Separately, no validation fold scores January at all — January 2025 is the first month of the training data, so nothing precedes it to train on; the protocol's W1 winter fold is the only retrospective instrument that looks at it.

Diagnostics 2 and 3 · adversarial test, reviewed 20 Sep 2026

Reweighting closes most of the gap — experimentally

If the two sets differ in which flights they hold, then reweighting validation toward the ranking set's input distribution should push the CV score toward the official one. Four classifiers were trained to tell 2025 departures from 2026 ranking departures, and the density ratio from one of them reweighted the saved out-of-fold losses.

An independent review found a weighting error. The ratio was estimated on July–December and then applied to both groups, but R_B_long holds only November and December and needs weights learned from its own months. The review's corrected figure was 269.40, against the 252.30 first reported. The tool has since been rebuilt to estimate each group's ratio from its own source months, cross-fit over both classes, and apply the source/target class-count correction per half; it now returns 267.58. All three are experimental diagnostics, not forecasts.

The balance check improves with the fix: group B's weighted no-flight-ID share moves from 0.99% to 1.484% against the ranking set's 1.532%, a residual of 0.05 percentage points. Airport mix is the worst remaining mismatch — LFPG sits 0.79 points low in group A — so the weights match the ranking set better without matching it.

What 269.40 is not
  • Not a validated forecast. The method has never been tested on a period where the answer is known.
  • Not fully balanced. Closer to ranking, not matched — airport mix is still off by up to 0.79 points. Better weights could move the number either way.
  • Concentrated. The top 1% of flights carry 56% of the weighted squared error, and ten flights alone are 12% of group A's and 26% of group B's increase.
  • Blind to weather, traffic and history — those columns are absent from the saved predictions it used.
  • No help for unseen stands. Weights cannot create validation examples for stands that never appear.
  • Built on six-month CV models, while the submitted model trained on twelve.

Four readings have to be resisted here. AUC measures separability, not cost — D3 sitting above D2 says an ordinary change of season separates as well as a change of year; it says nothing about how much either hurts taxi-out predictions. D0 does not prove there is no leakage: every D0 row is from 2025, so a year column would pass it while making D1 and D2 trivial. Calendar fields are excluded by construction, and that — not D0 — is the guard. Training gain is not evidence: stand and destination took 86% of the gain even in D0, where the model performs at chance, so gain was measuring cardinality. Held-out permutation importance has since replaced it, and it tells a different story — for the year contrast the strongest single column is runway (AUC drop 0.082), then stand (0.060), aircraft type (0.055), destination (0.042) and operator (0.041), a far flatter spread than gain implied; D0's largest drop is 0.002, which is the null behaving. And moderate weights still amplify a few flights — ten flights are 12% of group A's and 26% of group B's rise in mean squared error, which an effective sample size near 56% does not reveal.

What the test does support: the weights consistently raise CV error. Resampling calendar days with the weights held fixed gives an increase of +15.8 s, 95% interval [9.9, 22.2] — measured on the original weights, conditional on them being right.

Where this leaves the hypothesis

Mostly borne out, and mostly unproven

Supported by measurement
The row classes that produce catastrophic errors really are more prevalent in January 2026 — 1.72× overall, 2.75× at EHAM — and January is 44.3% of the ranking set and 0% of every validation fold. Inside validation, ordinary flights carry 77.7% of the squared error; on the leaderboard, 58 rows carried at least 17%. Those are counts and saved errors, and they stand.
Suggested, not established
That differences in which flights each set contains account for most of the 43.07 s gap. An experimental reweighting reaches 267.58, but the method is untested where the truth is known, and its balance against ranking proportions is imperfect.
Unexplained
The remaining ≈ 12 s. It is a remainder, not a located defect — nobody has shown it is error waiting to be fixed, it is not attributed to labels, and better weights, omitted features or unseen combinations could all move it.
What we are doing next

Two investigations, neither blocking the other

Testing whether reweighting works is worth doing, but it is not a gate on the original question — which flights the ranking set holds that training does not. The two run in parallel.

A · Training versus ranking — the original question

  1. A1
    Build the features the comparison is missing
    Weather, runway queue, stand clock and dirty-day inputs are not in the saved predictions the test used, and the control run carries only 3 of 21 weather and 3 of 11 queue columns. Building them for both sides is real work, and it is worth doing for the comparison itself — whatever happens to the reweighting.
  2. A2
    Unseen stands — measured on labelled flights
    In the July–December 2025 folds, unfamiliar stands showed no clear extra error: 1,291 flights at RMSE 225.5 against 249.9, a day-resampled difference of −24.4 s, 95% [−48.6, +3.4]. Unfamiliar stand/runway combinations had higher errors — 6,178 flights, 313.9 against 249.4, +64.5 s, 95% [+34.2, +96.8] — but a small overall effect: 0.416 s if they matched the rest, and 0.015 s for unfamiliar destinations. Rows carrying no usable key stay in the denominator rather than being dropped, which an earlier version of this page got wrong; there are 11 of them on the stand keys, at RMSE 2,457. These are 2025 folds, and they establish nothing about January 2026. Weights still cannot reach combinations absent from the source.
  3. A3
    Flag suspicious flights, and say that is what they are
    Without ranking labels we can flag flights whose predictions disagree with their surrogate clocks (LOBT, IOBT, EOBT1, AOBT3, schedule). A large prediction is not a large error. Whether unfamiliar stands actually produce larger errors has to be tested on historical flights whose labels we hold.

B · Does reweighting transport? — separate investigation

  1. B1
    One fixed model, five months it never trained on
    Holding out a month's errors is not enough: later A-fold models have already trained on that month's labels. The Jan–June model v3_2025-06 predicts July, September, October, November and December without having seen any of them. Take each in turn as the pretend ranking month, weight from the others, and compare against its measured RMSE — and against both the unweighted estimate and the simple no-flight-ID adjustment.
  2. B2
    Test ranking, not just level — and freeze first
    Predicting one model's score accurately does not show the method can tell which of two models is better. Test whether it gets candidate-minus-control differences right, and freeze the method before looking at results. These 2025 tests still leave January performance largely untested.
  3. B3
    Two separate checks, not one
    Comparing weighted flight proportions against the ranking set's checks whether the datasets match better. It does not check whether the classifier's probabilities are accurate — that needs its own calibration check.
  4. B4
    Keep the fixes the review asked for
    Source-specific weights for every group, held-out permutation importance in place of training gain, and error-concentration reporting beside effective sample size.
